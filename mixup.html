Context

Mixup is a SaaS built by the founders of Puremix. Think Google Docs for music — a shared workspace where audio engineers upload tracks, collect timestamped feedback, and manage versions with their clients.

The founder is a Grammy-nominated engineer who has worked with Shakira, Jennifer Lopez, and Mark Ronson. His credibility was the primary acquisition channel — the product had to live up to the brand.

The challenge

The product had one core complexity: designing for two completely different users sharing the same interface.

🎛 Audio engineers (admin) — upload, version, share, manage 🎤 Artists & clients (guest) — just listen and comment

A feature that gave engineers control couldn't confuse a guest who received a link and just wants to play the track.

What we built

Dashboard

The central hub where engineers manage all their projects — organized into folders, current and archived. A quickview panel previews sharing status, comment count, and version history without navigating away.

Before: a dense data table with no visual hierarchy. After: card layout with project status, quickview panel, and the neon-inspired dark theme built around studio aesthetics.

Player

The core workspace where collaboration happens. An engineer shares a link — the client opens it, listens, and leaves feedback.

  • Audio versioning — upload v1, v2, v3; all accessible and comparable
  • Timestamped comments — anchored to specific moments in the waveform
  • General comments — for overall feedback, separate from timestamp thread
  • Sharing modes — public link, password-protected, or private
  • Admin panel — permissions and settings, collapsible within the player view

Album

A workspace grouping multiple players — for sharing a full EP or album. Each player has its own featured version; the admin promotes one version per track to appear on the album view.

Design system

Built from scratch. The brand direction: neon lights in an audio studio. Dark background, vivid accent colors (teal, purple, amber) that evoke studio monitors and mixing console lighting. Designed to feel professional and music-native — not like a generic SaaS tool.

User testing

3 semi-directed scenarios · 5 participants · 6 prioritized outcomes

Profiles: Stéphan (audio engineer & teacher), Fab (audio engineer), Matis (producer), Maxime (producer), Franck (musician).

Pain points found before launch

01
Dashboard disorientationUsers got lost browsing files from the quickview — context was unclear
02
Player access frictionNobody double-clicked to open a player; the quickview → "Access player" path was too hidden
03
Terminology confusion"Player" was confused with "playlist"; "album" and "showcase" weren't intuitive
04
Comment readabilityNo visual hierarchy between timestamped and general feedback
05
Hidden scroll in version listUsers didn't realise the version list was scrollable; older versions invisible
06
Admin panel duplicationSeparate admin page felt redundant and confused users needing to take action

What we fixed

  • Single-click to open player from dashboard
  • Renamed terminology to match mental models
  • Redesigned comments with timestamp badges and threading
  • Added scroll indicators and version count to the version list
  • Collapsed admin settings into a side panel within the player, removing the standalone page

Takeaways

Designing for two users is designing two products.

The engineer and the guest have completely different mental models. Every screen had to work for both — constant tension between power and simplicity.

Terminology is UX.

The confusion between "player" and "playlist" showed that labels aren't just words — they're mental model anchors. Getting them wrong breaks the entire flow.

Stay vigilant about competitors.

The audio collaboration space moved fast. Reprioritizing based on what competitors were shipping was as important as internal user feedback.

Marketing and design are the same first impression.

Aligning the onboarding flow with the brand voice — from first ad to first login to first shared track — was one of the most impactful things we did for activation.
